Learn Facilitation with Phoebe · Session 3 of 6

Diverge, converge, decide

Most meetings do one of two things with a hard question: settle it in the first ten minutes on the opinions everyone already had, or argue it round in circles until the clock runs out. Both skip the part where a group actually thinks. Tonight we draw Kaner's Diamond of Participatory Decision-Making and find where groups stall, treat the uncomfortable middle as a stage to work through rather than a sign of failure, pick one tool for each zone, and name the rule that turns talk into a decision before anybody starts talking.

Part 1 · covers Kaner's Diamond of Participatory Decision-Making

The Diamond, and why groups close too early

5 min live

Kaner and colleagues draw a group's work on a new topic as a shape. In the 2014 figure the zones are labelled Business as Usual, Divergent Zone, Groan Zone, Convergent Zone and Closure Zone. The early rounds of any discussion cover familiar opinions, and most groups try to close right there (Kaner et al. 2014, p.9). For an easy problem that is fine. For a hard one, the answer was never among the familiar opinions, so closing early just means deciding without thinking.

LiveBusiness as usual, and when it is fine2 min▶

Business as usual is the thin line at the left of the drawing: someone raises a topic, three or four people say what they already think, a plausible answer appears, and the group takes it. Kaner's point is not that this is wrong. For an easy or familiar problem, the familiar opinions contain the answer, and opening a full diamond would waste everyone's afternoon. Choosing a venue for the team lunch does not need a groan zone.

The trouble is that groups use the same short path for hard problems, where the answer is not in anyone's starting position. Larkfield's two failed meetings (constructed) were business as usual: Ravi stated Platform's view, Mara stated Growth's, each restated it with more force, and the meeting tried to close on one of two familiar opinions. Neither side could accept the other's, so it never closed at all. The facilitator's first diagnostic question is simple: is this an easy problem or a hard one? If hard, plan the diamond.

LiveThe divergent zone: widen before you judge2 min▶

Kaner pairs divergent and convergent thinking as opposite behaviours (2014, p.6). Divergent thinking is generating alternatives, free-flowing open discussion, gathering diverse points of view, and suspending judgment. The single most important rule in this zone is the last one: an idea that is evaluated the moment it appears teaches everyone to offer only safe ideas.

At Larkfield the divergent question is not "which four items?" but "which combination of items would serve both teams?" That reframe invites options neither lead walked in with, such as an item that is platform work and also unblocks a growth experiment. The tool in Part 2 makes sure the quiet nine produce options before the two leads have framed the debate.

Self-studyThe convergent zone: judge, out loud and on purpose3 min▶

The convergent side of Kaner's pairs is evaluating alternatives, summarizing key points, sorting ideas into categories, and exercising judgment (2014, p.6). Notice that judgment is not banned in the diamond; it is scheduled. A facilitator who announces "we are now converging, so criticism is welcome" gives the room permission to do what it held back an hour earlier.

The commonest failure is mixing the two zones in one conversation: someone proposes, someone else immediately shoots it down, and the room learns that proposing is dangerous. Signal which zone the group is in, on the wall if possible, and move between them on purpose. Kaner offers the diamond for three uses: a lens for observing what a group is doing, a roadmap for designing an agenda, and shared language the group itself can use (2014, p.20). The third is the cheapest: draw it on the flipchart and the room can say "we are groaning" instead of "this is going nowhere".

Ask the easy-or-hard question at the agenda stage. For each item in your session 2 agenda, mark it E (familiar, business as usual is fine) or H (needs the full diamond). An H item with a ten-minute slot is a plan to close on familiar opinions.

Part 2 · covers the Groan Zone and a tool for each zone

The groan zone is normal

5 min live

Between the divergent and convergent zones there is a stretch where nobody understands anybody, people repeat themselves and patience runs short. Kaner presents this confusion and frustration as a natural part of group decision-making and a direct consequence of having different people in the room (2014, pp.18-21). In the book's words: "Building shared understanding is a struggle, not a platitude." (Kaner et al. 2014, p.20) A facilitator who treats the groan as a malfunction rushes the group straight back to the thin line.

LiveWhat the groan zone looks like2 min▶

Kaner describes the experience from the inside: struggling to understand ideas that are foreign or opposed to your own is unpleasant, and people become repetitive, defensive and short-tempered (2014, p.19). The room signs are recognisable. The same point is made a third time, a little louder. Someone says "as I already said". Side conversations start. A senior person sighs and checks the time.

None of that means the meeting has failed. It means the group has more than one frame of reference in it and is trying to fit them together, which is exactly what a hard problem needs. Kaner frames the groan zone as a direct, inevitable consequence of diversity in a group, and working through it is what builds agreements that last (2014, p.21).

LiveWhat a facilitator does there3 min▶

Name it. Kaner notes that simply naming the groan zone can help a group (2014, p.19). "This is the uncomfortable part. It means we have real differences on the table, and it is supposed to feel like this for a while." Naming turns a private sense of failure into a shared stage.

Paraphrase, then ask each side to restate the other. The facilitator paraphrases first, to model it. Then Ravi states Growth's reasoning until Mara says "yes, that is it", and Mara does the same for Platform. Nobody may argue until the restatement is accepted. This is session 1's mutual understanding, turned into a move.

Slow down rather than vote. The tempting escape from the groan zone is "let's just vote". A vote taken mid-confusion records who was more frustrated, not what the group thinks. Give it more time, or a smaller question, before anyone counts anything.

Self-studyThe worst moment for a boss to announce a decision2 min▶

Kaner's opening chapter names a specific failure: a person-in-charge who, seeing the confusion, steps in and announces a decision. The problem the book points to is poor timing (2014, p.16). The group was in the middle of the work, had not yet understood each other, and is now handed an answer. The result it describes is distrust and cynicism, because people learn that the struggle was optional and their contributions were not going to count.

At Larkfield (constructed) this is why Dana is not in the room (session 1). If Dana were present and impatient at 16:00, the groan zone is precisely where an announcement would land worst. The fallback exists, but it fires at 16:20, after the objections are on the record, not mid-groan.

Self-studyThe tools, zone by zone4 min▶

Divergent: 1-2-4-All (Lipmanowicz and McCandless, Liberating Structures). About 15 minutes: 1 minute to introduce the question, 1 minute alone in silence, 2 minutes in pairs, 5 minutes in foursomes, 7 minutes with the whole group sharing what stood out. It needs a group of at least four. Everyone writes before anyone speaks, so the options on the wall are not just the leads' options; with eleven people, Jo forms foursomes of 4, 4 and 3.

Groan: no structure, only moves. Naming, paraphrasing, restating the other side, slowing down. There is no tool that skips the groan zone, which is the point.

Convergent: clustering, then dot voting. Both are common practice rather than a cited method: group similar ideas on the wall, name each cluster in a few words the room agrees with, then let each person place a small number of dots to show where energy is. Dot voting shows a preference distribution. It does not make a decision, and the build-along says why.

Closure: a rule. The zone ends with a decision only if the group knows which rule makes one. That is Part 3.

Part 3 · covers decision rules and consent

Name the rule before the talking starts

5 min live

Kaner's index lists the common decision rules: unanimous agreement, majority vote, flip a coin, person-in-charge decides after discussion, and person-in-charge decides without discussion (2014, p.326 onward). Every one of them is legitimate for some decision. What is never legitimate is a rule chosen after the discussion, by whoever benefits from it, or no rule at all, in which case the meeting just stops and everyone leaves with a different idea of what was agreed.

LiveWhy the rule comes first2 min▶

A rule chosen after people know where the discussion is heading is not a rule, it is a tactic. If Platform is ahead on numbers, Platform will favour a vote; if Growth's lead has the sponsor's ear, Growth will favour "let Dana decide". Choosing the rule before anyone has argued for anything is the only moment it can be chosen on its merits. Kaner's index has an entry on the effects of decision rules on participation (2014, p.332), and the practical reading is simple: people contribute differently when they know whether their view will be counted, weighed or merely heard.

At Larkfield (constructed) the rule was set in session 1, in the contract with Dana: consent among the eleven, with a fallback at 16:20 in which Dana decides after hearing the two leads summarise the remaining objections. Jo says it out loud at 15:00 and again at 15:35, before the decision hour starts.

LiveConsent is not unanimity3 min▶

Unanimous agreement asks whether everyone agrees. Consent, as described in the Sociocracy 3.0 pattern by Bockelbrink, Priest and David, asks something different: does anyone have an objection? An objection is not a preference. It is a reason that reveals a risk worth avoiding or a worthwhile improvement to the proposal. A concern is not yet an objection; it can be noted without blocking. A proposal goes ahead when it is good enough for now and safe enough to try, until the next review.

That makes consent fit a decision like Larkfield's: reversible next quarter, with a review date, among people who each need only to be able to live with it. Kaner also distinguishes consensus from unanimity (indexed 2014, p.334), and warns that silence is not an agreement (indexed p.335). Consent handles the silence problem by asking each person in turn rather than asking the room "any objections?" and taking the pause as a yes.

Self-studyThe other rules, and a scale for agreement3 min▶

Majority vote is fast and familiar, and it produces a losing side that still has to implement the result. Person-in-charge decides after discussion (the "decider" in the chooser) is legitimate when announced first, as session 1 covered. Person-in-charge decides without discussion is also on Kaner's list and is right for some decisions, such as an emergency, as long as nobody pretends a discussion is shaping it. Flip a coin leaves the outcome to chance; it sounds absurd and can be honest when two options are genuinely equal and the cost is in delay.

Kaner's index also lists a gradients of agreement scale (2014, p.338): a way to ask people to place their support along a range from wholehearted to opposed, rather than a yes or no. The exact points on that scale were not read here, so they are not reproduced; session 6 returns to it as a closure tool.

Some summaries of Kaner add further rules to the list. Only the five above are confirmed in the index of the 2014 edition, so only those five are taught as Kaner's.

Real world

The meeting that "agreed" because nobody objected aloud. A familiar pattern: the chair summarises a proposal, asks "any objections?", waits two seconds, and says "great, agreed". A week later half the room says they never agreed, and they are right: they were thinking, or deferring to a senior person, or did not want to be the one to hold things up. Silence was recorded as consent. The fix is procedural, not personal: name the rule before the discussion, then ask each person by name for objections, and count only what was said.

Group decision quality and calibration sit in Decision Intelligence ↗; this session is about how the room reaches a decision, not whether the decision was good.

Build-along

Larkfield's decision hour, as a run sheet

★ 22 min · everyone builds

From 15:35 to 16:20 Jo walks the eleven people at Larkfield (constructed) through the whole diamond, ending in a consent round. Every time and script below is constructed. Write the same six steps for the decision part of a meeting of your own, filling in your question, your people and your clock times; if you have none coming up, use Larkfield.

Diverge with 1-2-4-All on a question both sides can answer. The question matters more than the tool. "Which four items?" invites two camps; "which combination serves both teams?" invites options. Jo reads the timings out loud so the clock is shared.

★ Step 1 · 15:35-15:50 · 1-2-4-All (constructed times)Question on the wall: "Which combination of items would serve BOTH teams next quarter, and why?" 1 min Jo introduces the question and the timings 1 min alone, in silence: write your ideas, one per note 2 min pairs: build on each other's ideas 5 min foursomes (4, 4 and 3 for eleven people): pick the ideas that stand out 7 min whole group: each foursome shares ONE idea that stood out; Jo posts every note Your version: question ____ people ____ start time ____

Cluster and name the options on the wall. The notes become a small number of candidate combinations. The group, not Jo, names each cluster; Jo only asks "what would you call this group of notes?" and writes the answer exactly.

★ Step 2 · 15:50-15:57 · cluster and nameJo: "Let's put notes that say the same thing next to each other. Anyone can move a note. If you disagree with where one went, move it back and say why." Then, per cluster: "What would you call this group?" (write their words) Output: three to five named options, each a combination of items, visible to everyone.

Work the groan zone: Ravi and Mara restate each other. The moment the leads start repeating themselves, Jo names the zone and switches to restatement. Nobody argues until each side has accepted the other's summary of its reasoning.

★ Step 3 · 15:57-16:05 · the groan zone scriptJo: "We have hit the uncomfortable part. That is normal; it means real differences are on the table." Jo: "Ravi, before you argue for anything, tell us why Growth wants option B. Mara, tell Ravi if he got it." (repeat until Mara says: yes, that is it) Jo: "Mara, now the same for Platform and option A." (repeat until Ravi says: yes, that is it) Jo: "Has anything changed in how you see the options?" If it is still circling: slow down, do NOT vote. Narrow the question: "which ONE item is the sticking point?"

Converge with a dot vote, as a straw poll and nothing more. The dots show where the room's energy is so a proposal can be drafted. Jo says before anyone votes that the dots decide nothing: a dot vote is a majority-style count, and the agreed rule is consent. Treating the dots as the decision would quietly swap the rule mid-meeting, which is exactly what naming it first was meant to prevent.

★ Step 4 · 16:05-16:08 · straw pollJo: "Three dots each (my choice, not a rule). Put them on the options you could most support. This is a straw poll. It does NOT decide anything. Our rule is consent, and the dots only tell us which option to draft as a proposal." Output: one proposal, drafted from the most-dotted option, written on the wall as four items in order.

Run the consent round. Jo reads the proposal, then asks each of the eleven by name. Every reply is either "no objection", a concern (noted, not blocking), or an objection with its reason. Jo tests each objection, and the group amends the proposal to resolve it.

★ Step 5 · 16:08-16:18 · consent roundJo reads the proposal aloud, word for word. Jo, to each person in turn, by name: "Do you have an objection to this proposal?" Test any objection: "What risk does it reveal, or what would it improve?" Names a risk or an improvement = objection: amend "I'd prefer something else" = concern: note it Amend: "What change would resolve that objection?" Then read the amended proposal and run the round again. Stop rule: good enough for now, safe enough to try, until the review date.

Record the decision and the rule that made it, or trigger the fallback. The record names the rule as well as the result, so nobody can later say "we never agreed". If 16:20 arrives with an objection still open, the fallback from the contract applies, and Jo has the objections already written down.

★ Step 6 · 16:18-16:20 · record or fallbackDecided by: consent among the eleven, at ____ Decision: items ____, ____, ____, ____ (in order) Concerns: ____ (noted, not blocking) Review date: ____ OR, at 16:20 with an objection open: Jo: "We have reached the fallback we agreed at 15:00." Dana joins; Ravi and Mara each summarise the remaining objections; Dana decides after hearing them. Record: "Decided by: Dana, after discussion (fallback)"

Say the rule three times. In the contract, at the start of the meeting, and immediately before the consent round. The third time is the one that stops a straw poll being mistaken for the decision.

Homework

Before session 4

30 min
  1. Draw one real meeting on the Diamond. Pick a recent discussion and mark where it started, where it groaned (if it did), and where it closed. Did it close on the thin line?
  2. Find the rule that was actually used. For one decision you were part of, write the rule that really made it. Was it named before the discussion, after, or never?
  3. Practise the restatement. In one disagreement this week, restate the other person's reasoning until they say "yes, that is it", before arguing your own. Note what changed.
  4. Finish your run sheet. All six steps with your own question, people and clock times, and the rule plus fallback written at the top.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 3 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Forty minutes in, people are repeating themselves and two leads are getting short with each other. What should the facilitator do?

Kaner presents the groan zone as a natural stage and notes that naming it can help. A vote mid-confusion counts frustration, and an announcement from the boss at that moment is the poor timing Kaner warns breeds distrust. Restatement builds the mutual understanding that lets the group converge.

2 · What is the difference between consent and unanimous agreement?

In the Sociocracy 3.0 pattern an objection reveals a risk worth avoiding or a worthwhile improvement; a preference or concern is not yet an objection. C is the silence trap: Kaner indexes "silence is not an agreement" (p.335), which is why consent asks each person in turn.

3 · Jo runs a dot vote at 16:05 and option B gets the most dots. Why is that not the decision?

The dots are a straw poll that shows where to draft a proposal. The rule was named first precisely so it cannot be swapped once people can see who it favours. The proposal from option B still goes through the consent round.
