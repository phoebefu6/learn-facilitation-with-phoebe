Learn Facilitation with Phoebe · Session 5 of 6

When the room goes wrong

Every facilitator meets the same four moments sooner or later: one voice that will not give up the floor, a question that lands in silence, a second meeting starting quietly in the corner, and two people whose disagreement has started to sound personal. The instinct is to deal with the person. The better first move is almost always to change the structure, because the structure is what let the pattern happen, and a new structure fixes it for everyone without singling anyone out. Tonight we build the escalation ladder from a round to a private word, read silence for what it is, treat side talk as information, and keep heat on the items instead of the people.

Part 0

Where we start

The airtime bench in session 4 showed a floor that tilts with nobody misbehaving: across 40 real AMI meetings the project manager held 37.6 percent of the airtime on average and was the top speaker in 30 of the 40 (measured). Nobody in those rooms was being difficult. The role told them to run the meeting, and running the meeting turned into holding the floor. That is the rule for tonight: when a room goes wrong, change the structure before you address the person. At Larkfield (constructed), the 90-minute workshop has been running for forty minutes, the decision hour has just begun, and at 15:40 things start to slide.

Kaner's guide gives two chapters to this ground, "Dealing with Difficult Dynamics" (ch.13) and "Classic Facilitator Challenges" (ch.14). Their titles were read in the publisher's table of contents; their text was not, so none of the moves below is attributed to them. The moves are common facilitation practice, and they are labelled that way. Two neighbours sit just outside the frame: difficult learners in a training room are handled in Course Design, Delivery 3, and a disagreement between two people one-to-one is in Communication, session 6.

Part 1 · covers the dominator

One voice keeps the floor

5 min live

At 15:40 Ravi, the Platform lead (Larkfield, constructed), starts his third long turn on item 3. Nothing he says is wrong. He knows the system better than anyone, and he is answering a question before anyone else has had the chance. Five minutes later the room has heard Platform's case for item 3 three times and Growth's case not at all. The escalation ladder is how Jo deals with it without making Ravi the problem.

LiveChange the structure before you address the person3 min▶

The bench result from session 4 is the reason for the order. In the AMI meetings the person holding the project manager role took 37.6 percent of the airtime on average, against 25 percent for an equal share of four, and was top speaker in 30 of 40 meetings (measured). The role asked them to run the meeting. Open discussion rewards whoever is fastest, most senior or most fluent, and that person usually has no idea they are holding the floor. Telling them so, in front of the room, treats a structural problem as a character flaw.

A structural move fixes the pattern without naming anyone. A round gives every person one turn in order before open discussion. Write first gives everyone two silent minutes to put their view on a card before anyone speaks. 1-2-4-All (Lipmanowicz and McCandless, read) runs about fifteen minutes: one minute alone, two in pairs, five in foursomes, seven with the whole group, and needs at least four people. A timebox per speaker ("one minute each, I will hold the clock") does the same in a smaller space. All four change who gets to speak next, and none of them says anything about Ravi.

Self-studyThe four rungs, and what each one costs4 min▶

Rung 1, structure. Costs nobody anything. Use it first and use it often; Jo can switch to a round at any moment by saying "let's go round, one minute each, starting with Mara's end of the table". Starting the round away from the dominant voice matters: the first answer becomes the reference point for everyone after.

Rung 2, the general invitation. "Let's hear from someone who has not spoken on this yet." It names nobody and costs nobody face, but it only works if the quiet people believe the invitation. Wait after saying it. If nobody takes it, go back down to rung 1 rather than up to rung 3: a round makes the invitation real.

Rung 3, the specific acknowledgement and redirect. "Ravi, I have your point on item 3 on the wall; I want Growth's view." Two halves, both needed. The acknowledgement tells Ravi he has been heard, which is usually why he keeps repeating himself; the redirect moves the floor. Point at where his point is written as you say it.

Rung 4, the private word. At the break, one to one, framed as a request for help rather than a complaint: "You know this system better than anyone, and I need Growth's view in the room before we decide. Can you help me by holding your points until the round?" Never in front of the group. If Jo is reaching for rung 4 in the first hour, something on rungs 1 and 2 was skipped.

Real world

The senior person who answers first. A familiar pattern in any organisation: the most senior person in the room answers every question before anyone else has drawn breath. They are not trying to dominate; they are trying to be helpful, and their answer is often good. But once the senior answer is on the table, everyone else either agrees with it or keeps quiet. The structural fix is to have everyone write their answer before the senior person speaks, then read the cards first and the senior view last.

Part 2 · covers silence and side conversations

A quiet room is not an agreeing room

5 min live

At 15:48 at Larkfield (constructed), Jo asks the room what they think of item 6, the data item, and nobody answers. The data lead, invited for exactly this item, looks at the table. At the far end two Growth team members are talking quietly to each other. Both signals look like the room moving on. Neither one is.

LiveSilence after a question is not a yes3 min▶

Kaner's index carries an entry for the idea that silence is not agreement (p.335). A quiet room can mean people agree. It can equally mean they have not understood the question, do not feel safe disagreeing with someone senior, think the decision has already been made, or are still thinking. A facilitator who reads silence as consent closes the item, and finds out next week that half the room never agreed.

Four moves, cheapest first. Wait longer than is comfortable. Most facilitators fill the gap within a few seconds; count slowly to ten in your head before saying anything. Ask everyone to write first. Two silent minutes, one card each, then read the cards before any discussion. Pairs before the whole room. Two minutes with a neighbour; saying a half-formed thought to one person is far easier than to eleven. Ask a specific person a specific question they can answer. Not "what do you think?", which asks for an opinion in public, but a question that draws on what they know: "From the data side, what would item 6 need before Growth could use it?"

Self-studySide conversations are data3 min▶

When two people start talking quietly at one end of the table, the usual reading is rudeness. A more useful reading, and usually the right one, is that someone has a point they do not feel able to make to the whole room: a doubt about the item, a fact that contradicts the speaker, a worry they want to test on a friend first. The side conversation is the meeting's missing contribution, happening somewhere the room cannot hear it.

So invite it in, warmly and without any edge: "Sounds like there's a point at that end, can we have it?" Said with curiosity, it tells the pair their thought is wanted. Never shame ("are we keeping you?"): it gets the conversation to stop, and it also teaches the whole room that raising a doubt is risky, which produces more silence later. If the pair says "nothing, sorry", accept it and move on; the invitation has done its job, and the point often arrives in the next round.

Design for the quiet ones before the room starts. If the agenda from session 2 puts write-first or a round at the start of every decision item, most silences never happen. The moves above are for the moment the plan did not cover.

Part 3 · covers conflict

Heat is not the problem

5 min live

At 15:56 at Larkfield (constructed), Ravi and Mara are back where the first two meetings ended: Ravi says item 3 has to go first, Mara says item 5 cannot wait, and both are getting louder. Disagreement is not the failure here. The Groan Zone from session 3 is the normal, expected stretch where different views collide, and Kaner presents it as an inevitable consequence of diversity in a group (ch.1, pp.18-21). The facilitator's job is to keep the heat on the items and off the people.

LiveKeep it about the items3 min▶

Paraphrase each position until its owner agrees. Jo turns to Ravi: "Let me check I have it. You're saying item 3 has to come first because the payments service is already close to its limit, and anything Growth ships on top makes that worse. Is that it?" If Ravi corrects anything, Jo paraphrases again. Then the same for Mara. Most heat comes from feeling unheard; a person who has just agreed with your summary of their view has much less reason to repeat it louder.

Separate what each side needs from what each side proposes. This is common practice in facilitation and negotiation, with no single citation behind it. The proposals (item 3 first, item 5 first) cannot both win. The needs behind them (a service that holds under load, a pricing test live this quarter) often can, and an option that serves both, here an item that makes payments observable and lets the test run on it (constructed), only becomes visible once the needs are written side by side.

Park what cannot be solved today, on a visible list. Ravi raises next year's hiring plan; Mara raises how Growth's targets were set. Both are real, neither is tonight's decision. Jo writes each on a sheet titled "Parked" in Ravi's and Mara's own words, says who will take each one forward, and returns to the items. A visible list is what makes parking credible: people let go of a point they can see has been kept.

Self-studyWhen to stop the meeting3 min▶

Two situations mean the meeting should not continue as planned. A personal attack: once the disagreement is about someone's competence or motives rather than an item, more process will not repair it in the room. A missing decider: if it becomes clear the person or rule that makes the decision final is not present, or the sponsor would not stand behind the result, the room is about to decide something that will not hold.

In both cases, say so plainly and reconvene: "I'm going to stop us here. We can't make a good decision in the next twenty minutes, and I'd rather we come back to it on Thursday than decide something that doesn't stick." Then say what happens next and when. Stopping a meeting is a process decision, which is exactly the facilitator's to make.

A facilitator is not a mediator. Formal conflict between two people, with history and stakes outside the meeting, is out of scope here and needs a different role. For the one-to-one conversation that sometimes follows, see Communication, session 6.

Real world

The two leads who have had this argument before. Larkfield, constructed: Ravi and Mara have argued about item 3 against item 5 in both earlier meetings, and the nine others have learned to wait it out. The heat is not new information; it is a script both of them know. What breaks the script is not asking them to calm down, but asking each to say, in one sentence, what they would need to be true to support the other's item. That question has no answer in the old script.

Say the move, not the problem. In every step Jo announces what the room will do next ("we'll go round", "can we have it", "let me check I have it") and never what someone did wrong. A group follows a clear instruction far more readily than it accepts a diagnosis.

Homework

Before session 6

30 min
  1. Run the checklist in someone else's meeting. Sit in a meeting you are not running and tick the five signals every ten minutes. Note the time each first appeared and what, if anything, the chair did.
  2. Write your four rungs. For a person you know tends to hold the floor, write the actual words you would use on each rung of the ladder, starting with the structural move you would try first.
  3. Rewrite one open question. Take a question you have asked a room recently that met silence. Rewrite it as a specific question for a specific person, drawing on what they know.
  4. Find the needs behind one disagreement. For a disagreement you have watched recently, write each side's proposal and, next to it, the need behind it. Is there an option that serves both needs?
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 5 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Twenty minutes into a decision item, one senior person has taken most of the airtime and is starting the same point again. What is the best first move?

Structure before the person. A round changes who speaks next for everyone and names nobody. Session 4's bench showed the floor tilting with nobody misbehaving: the project manager held 37.6 percent of airtime on average, partly because the role asked them to run the meeting. A public correction or a private word are higher rungs, used only when the structural move has been tried.

2 · Jo asks the room "any thoughts on item 6?" and nobody answers. What does the silence tell Jo?

Silence is not agreement (Kaner indexes the idea, p.335), and it is not reliably disagreement either. A, B and C each read one meaning into it. Writing first, pairs, or a specific question to a specific person turn the silence into answers.

3 · Two people at the end of the table have been talking quietly while others speak. What should the facilitator do?

A side conversation is usually a point someone does not feel able to say to the room. Inviting it in gets that point into the meeting. Shaming stops the talk, and teaches everyone that raising a doubt is risky. If the pair declines, accept it and move on.
