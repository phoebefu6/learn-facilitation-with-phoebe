Learn Facilitation with Phoebe · Session 1 of 6

Own the process, not the answer

Every meeting has two things going on at once: what the group is deciding, and how it is deciding it. Most meetings let whoever cares most about the first quietly run the second. A facilitator is the person who takes the second job on purpose and, in exchange, gives up any claim on the first. Tonight we separate the two jobs, turn Kaner's four participatory values into four things a facilitator actually does, sort out the three hats nobody can wear at once, and hold the conversation with the sponsor that decides most of what happens before anyone enters the room.

Part 0

Where we start

The running case is constructed. Larkfield is an invented software company where two teams, Platform and Growth, must agree one shared roadmap for next quarter: eight candidate items and room for about four. They have tried twice. Both meetings ended with Ravi, who leads Platform, and Mara, who leads Growth, debating across the table while nine other people watched and nobody decided anything. Jo, from a third team with no stake in the roadmap, has been asked to facilitate a 90-minute workshop. Everything Jo does across the six sessions is a move you can make in your own meetings.

Part 1 · covers process against content

Two jobs at one table

5 min live

Content is what the group decides: which four roadmap items go ahead. Process is how it decides: who speaks and when, the order of the items, how long each gets, and what rule turns a discussion into a decision. Larkfield's first two meetings had plenty of content and no process at all, so the process defaulted to "the two loudest people argue until the clock runs out".

LiveProcess is a job, and someone always does it3 min▶

No meeting is free of process. If nobody designs it, the room still has one: the most senior or most fluent person sets the order, the first idea on the table becomes the reference point, and the decision rule is whatever the most powerful person does when the clock runs out. Larkfield's two failed meetings were not unprocessed. They ran on that default, and the default produced exactly what it usually produces, a debate between two leads and nine spectators.

The facilitator's offer is to take that job out of the hands of whoever happens to be loudest and do it deliberately. Jo will decide the order of items, who speaks in what pattern, how long each part gets, and, with the sponsor, which rule turns talk into a decision. Jo will not decide which four items go ahead, and will not hint.

Self-study"Content-neutral": whose phrase, and what it does not mean3 min▶

The phrase "content-neutral" is usually credited to Michael Doyle, of the Doyle and Straus tradition of meeting facilitation (reported; Doyle also wrote the foreword to the third edition of Kaner's guide). Kaner's book sits in the same tradition and frames the role around supporting everyone to do their best thinking. The quote is Doyle's, not Kaner's, and it is worth getting right because the two are often merged.

Neutral about content is not passive about process. A facilitator interrupts, reorders, stops a debate that has gone round twice, asks a quiet person directly, and refuses to let a vote happen before the rule is agreed. All of that is process. What neutrality forbids is the small stuff that leaks a preference: paraphrasing one side more generously, writing one person's point bigger on the wall, or asking a leading question ("do we really think item 6 is worth it?").

Real world

The manager who "just facilitates". A familiar pattern in any organisation: the team lead runs the workshop, asks for everyone's ideas, writes them up, and then closes with "great, so I think we go with option two". The group learns that the process was decoration around a decision already made, and next time contributes less. Nothing in that sequence is malicious. It is two hats worn at once, which Part 3 deals with.

Part 2 · covers Kaner's four participatory values

Four values, four jobs

5 min live

Kaner and colleagues' Facilitator's Guide to Participatory Decision-Making (third edition, 2014) grounds the role in four participatory values: full participation, mutual understanding, inclusive solutions and shared responsibility. The useful part is that the book turns each value into a facilitator function, so a value stops being a poster and becomes something you do in the room.

LiveFull participation and mutual understanding3 min▶

Full participation is the one most often reduced to "everyone gets a chance to speak". The function is bigger: the facilitator builds structures in which people who would not speak in an open debate do. A round where each person speaks once in turn, writing before talking, pairs before the whole group. Session 4 measures how unequal real meetings are without those structures, and session 5 has the moves for when one person keeps the floor.

Mutual understanding means each side can state the other side's position well enough that the other side agrees with the summary. At Larkfield, Platform thinks Growth wants features shipped on an unstable base; Growth thinks Platform wants a quarter of invisible work. Neither has heard the other's actual reasoning. Jo's move is to paraphrase across the table and ask the other team to confirm or correct, before anyone evaluates anything.

Self-studyInclusive solutions and shared responsibility3 min▶

Inclusive solutions are options that take account of everyone's interests instead of one side winning. They rarely appear in the first round of ideas, which is why Kaner's model, session 3, insists on a stretch of divergence before anyone converges. At Larkfield, an item that makes the payments service observable might be platform work that also unblocks a growth experiment; it only surfaces if someone asks what each side needs, not which items each side wants.

Shared responsibility means the group owns the outcome and its follow-through, not the facilitator and not the sponsor. The practical form is in session 6: owners named in the room, by the group, with dates, and a record everyone saw being written.

Use the values as a design check. Before any session, read your plan against the four: where in this agenda does every person speak at least once, where does each side hear the other restated, where is there room for an option nobody brought in, and where do owners get chosen? A gap in any of the four is a gap in the plan.

Part 3 · covers the three hats

Facilitator, participant, decider: one hat at a time

5 min live

There are three roles in a decision meeting: the person who runs the process, the people who contribute content, and the person or rule that makes the decision final. One person can hold more than one of these across a week. In any given minute, they can hold only one honestly.

LiveWhen the boss has to run it3 min▶

Sometimes there is no neutral person available, and the manager who will make the call also has to run the meeting. That works when the decision rule is said out loud before discussion starts. Kaner's index lists "person-in-charge decides after discussion" as one of the common decision rules, next to unanimous agreement and majority vote; it is a legitimate rule, not a failure of participation. What damages trust is a different sequence: inviting the group to decide, letting them believe they are deciding, and then announcing a decision. Kaner's opening chapter names poor timing of that announcement, in the middle of confusion, as a source of distrust and cynicism.

The honest version sounds like: "I will make this call on Friday. Today I need your best thinking on the trade-offs, and I will tell you what I decided and why." The group then knows its job is to inform, not to decide, and contributes accordingly.

Self-studySwitching hats out loud2 min▶

A facilitator who has real knowledge the group needs can step out of the role briefly, if they say so: "I am stepping out of the facilitator role for a minute to share something from the security review, then stepping back in." Physically moving away from the wall or putting the marker down helps the room track it. Doing it often is a sign the wrong person is facilitating.

Jo has no stake at Larkfield, which is why Jo was asked. If Jo did know something material, for example that one roadmap item depends on a vendor contract Jo's team is negotiating, the right move is to say so in the contracting conversation below, before the workshop, and let the sponsor decide whether Jo is still the right person.

Build-along

The sponsor contract, on one page

★ 22 min · everyone builds

Most of what goes wrong in a workshop was decided before it started: nobody agreed who makes the final call, what the group is allowed to change, or what happens if it does not decide. Jo spends twenty minutes with Dana, Larkfield's head of product and the sponsor (constructed), and writes the answers down. Write the same page for a meeting of your own as you go; if you have none coming up, use Larkfield.

Write the decision as a question with a finite answer. "Talk about the roadmap" is a topic. A decision has an answer you could write on one line. Jo asks Dana for the question until it passes that test.

★ Step 1 · the decisionWhich four of the eight candidate items will Platform and Growth commit to as one shared roadmap for next quarter? Test: could the answer fit on one line? "items 2, 3, 5, 8" yes

Name who decides and by what rule. This is the line most often skipped. Session 3 has the full menu of rules; for now, write one down and the fallback if the first rule does not produce an answer.

★ Step 2 · decider and ruleRule: the eleven people in the room decide by consent (go ahead unless someone raises a reasoned objection) Fallback: if no consent by 16:20, Dana decides after hearing the two leads summarise the remaining objections Said out loud at 15:00, before any discussion.

Describe the outcome you will hold up at the end. A noun, not an activity. Session 2 builds the whole agenda backwards from this.

★ Step 3 · the outcomeA one-page list: four items, in order, each with one owner from either team and a first milestone date. Seen and confirmed by everyone before they leave.

Decide who must be in the room, and who must not. Everyone whose work changes, and everyone who could block the decision afterwards. Dana should not attend the discussion if the rule is consent among the eleven: her presence changes who speaks.

★ Step 4 · the roomIn: both leads, all nine team members, Jo Out: Dana (joins at 16:20 only if the fallback is triggered) Ask: is anyone missing who could undo this next week? (Answer at Larkfield: the data lead, invited for item 6)

List the givens. Things the group cannot change, said before discussion, so nobody spends twenty minutes arguing about them.

★ Step 5 · givens- Capacity is about four items; no new hires this quarter - Item 1 (the compliance deadline) is already committed and is NOT one of the four - Ranking criteria are open; the candidate list is fixed

Agree what happens if the group does not decide. A meeting without this line drifts past its end time. With it, everyone knows the clock is real.

★ Step 6 · if no decisionIf there is no consent by 16:20, the fallback in step 2 applies. Jo records the remaining objections word for word so Dana decides on the group's reasoning, not on memory.

Read it back to the sponsor. Jo reads all six lines back to Dana and asks one question: "If the room decides this, will you stand behind it?" A sponsor who hesitates has told you the real decider is someone else, and that is far cheaper to learn now than at 16:20.

Homework

Before session 2

30 min
  1. Watch one meeting for process only. Write down who set the order, who spoke first on each item, and what actually ended each discussion. Name the decision rule the meeting really used.
  2. Finish your contract. All six lines for a real meeting you will run or attend. If line 2 is empty, that is the finding.
  3. Find one leaked preference. In a meeting you ran recently, recall one moment you nudged the content. What process move would have done the job without the nudge?
  4. Bring the outcome line from step 3. Session 2 builds the agenda backwards from it.
Check yourself

Three questions

Clear all three and session 1 gets stamped in your passport.

1 · Halfway through the Larkfield workshop, Jo thinks item 6 is clearly the weakest. What should Jo do?

A facilitator's opinion on content turns them into a participant with control of the process, the worst combination. C and D are the same leak in disguise: a layout choice and a leading question both carry a preference. A neutral question that makes everyone examine every item does the job.

2 · Which statement about "content-neutral" is accurate?

The phrase is reported as Doyle's, from the tradition Kaner's book belongs to. Neutral about what is decided, active about how: interrupting a debate that has gone round twice is process work. Subject knowledge is fine; using it to steer is not.

3 · A team lead must run a meeting and will also make the final call. What keeps that honest?

"Person-in-charge decides after discussion" is one of the decision rules Kaner lists; it is legitimate when it is announced. B and C are the sequence that breeds cynicism: inviting a group to decide and then announcing a decision.
