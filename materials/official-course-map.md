## Running case (constructed, say so on every page that uses it)

**Larkfield** is a constructed mid-size software company. Two teams, **Platform** (6 people, lead: Ravi)
and **Growth** (5 people, lead: Mara), must agree ONE shared roadmap for next quarter: eight candidate
items, capacity for about four. They have tried twice in a normal meeting and it ended both times with
Mara and Ravi debating while nine people watched. **Jo**, a facilitator from a third team with no stake
in the roadmap, is asked to run a 90-minute workshop. Every page that uses Larkfield labels it
"constructed". All names are invented. No number about Larkfield is a measurement.

## Verified facts (cite exactly as here)

### Kaner et al. 2014 (read: Wiley page, publisher TOC, ch.1 excerpt, index)
- Kaner, S., with Lind, L., Toldi, C., Fisk, S., and Berger, D. (2014). *Facilitator's Guide to
  Participatory Decision-Making* (3rd ed.). Jossey-Bass / Wiley. 432 pp. First edition 1996 (reported:
  New Society Publishers), second 2007 (Jossey-Bass).
- Diamond zones as labelled in the 2014 figure: Business as Usual, Divergent Zone, Groan Zone, Convergent
  Zone, Closure Zone. The Groan Zone is presented as a normal, inevitable consequence of diversity in a
  group; naming it can help the group (ch.1, pp.18-21).
- Most groups try to close a discussion after the early rounds of "familiar opinions" (p.9).
- Four participatory values: full participation, mutual understanding, inclusive solutions, shared
  responsibility; the facilitator's four functions mirror them (encourage full participation, promote
  mutual understanding, foster inclusive solutions, cultivate shared responsibility; index pp.33-36).
- Decision rules indexed (p.326 onward): unanimous agreement, majority vote, flip a coin, person-in-charge
  decides after discussion, person-in-charge decides without discussion. "Silence is not an agreement"
  (indexed p.335). A gradients of agreement scale is indexed (p.338). "Delegation" NOT verified.
- Agendas: chapters 10-12 "Effective Agendas" (design principles, desired outcomes, process design).
- "Content-neutral" is Michael Doyle's phrase (reported), the tradition Kaner's book sits in (Doyle wrote
  the 3rd edition foreword: read on the catalogue record).

### Consent (read)
- Sociocracy 3.0 pattern "Consent Decision-Making" (Bockelbrink, Priest, David; CC BY-SA 4.0):
  invite objections; an objection reveals a risk worth avoiding or a worthwhile improvement; a concern
  is not yet an objection; proposals go ahead when "good enough for now and safe enough to try" until
  the next review.

### Liberating Structures 1-2-4-All (read)
- Lipmanowicz and McCandless. About 15 min: 1 min intro, 1 min alone, 2 min pairs, 5 min foursomes,
  7 min whole group. Minimum group of four. Do NOT repeat the Franklin quote on that page (misattribution).

### Google Project Aristotle (read) and Duhigg (read via archive)
- re:Work "Understand team effectiveness": 180 teams (115 engineering project teams, 65 sales pods);
  five dynamics in order: psychological safety, dependability, structure and clarity, meaning, impact.
  The words "turn" and "conversation" do not appear on the page. Not significant at Google: colocation,
  consensus-driven decision making, extroversion, individual performance, workload size, seniority,
  team size, tenure.
- Duhigg, C. (2016). "What Google Learned From Its Quest to Build the Perfect Team." NYT Magazine,
  28 Feb 2016. Brings Woolley's turn-taking finding into the Google story.
- **Teach:** "Google found equal turn-taking makes teams effective" is a conflation.

### The collective-intelligence disagreement (teach it, do not resolve it)
- Woolley, Chabris, Pentland, Hashmi, Malone (2010), Science 330(6004):686-688: 699 people, groups of
  2-5, two studies; a "c factor" not strongly correlated with average or maximum member intelligence;
  correlated with average social sensitivity, equality in distribution of conversational turn-taking, and
  proportion of females. (read: abstract). Effect sizes not verified: never quote one.
- Bates and Gupta (2017), Intelligence 60:46-56: 3 studies, 312 people; individual IQ accounted for
  around 80% of group-IQ differences; turn-taking and number of women hypotheses not supported. (read)
- Credé and Howardson (2017), J Applied Psychology 102(10):1483-1492, "The structure of group task
  performance - A second look at 'collective intelligence'": 6 samples reanalysed; support "generally
  weak". (read: abstract)
- Riedl, Kim, Gupta, Malone, Woolley (2021), PNAS 118(21) e2005737118: 22 studies, 5,279 individuals,
  1,356 groups; robust CI factor; process more important than member skill overall; but "process" there
  is skill congruence, strategy and effort on an online platform, NOT turn-taking. (read)
- **Line for the page:** equal airtime as a CAUSE of smarter groups is not settled. The facilitation case
  for balancing airtime does not need it: a decision that only one voice shaped has only that voice's
  information in it, and the people who were not heard do not own it (Kaner's participatory values).

### AMI Meeting Corpus (read: licence, overview, scenario page, and the data itself)
- CC BY 4.0 (LICENCE.txt in the annotation zip and the licence page). Attribution: Carletta, J. et al.
  (2006). The AMI Meeting Corpus: A Pre-announcement. MLMI 2005, LNCS, Springer, pp. 28-39,
  doi:10.1007/11677482_3 (LNCS volume 3869 reported). Data: groups.inf.ed.ac.uk/ami. Modified: we ship
  segment start, length and speaker only.
- 100 hours; about two-thirds scenario meetings; four people play project manager (PM), marketing
  expert (ME), user interface designer (UI) and industrial designer (ID) designing a TV remote control,
  four meetings per team in one day: kick-off, functional, conceptual, detailed design. Participants were
  not trained designers. Mostly non-native English speakers. The PM role is defined as coordinating and
  running the meetings: the role ASKS the PM to talk. Teach that.

## Bench canon (python == node, 579 values identical, `python3 materials/build-ami-airtime.py --json .` vs `node materials/airtime-node.js .`)

Sample: 32 eligible scenario teams (four meetings, four roles, timings complete; TS3009 excluded for
known timing gaps), 10 drawn with seed 20261006: ES2004, ES2008, ES2009, ES2011, IS1001, IS1003,
IS1008, TS3006, TS3008, TS3012. 40 meetings, 21,166 speaker segments, ami-sample.js 203 KB.

Definitions (state them on the page): airtime = union of a person's segments; share = airtime / sum of
the four; Gini over the four shares (0 = equal, 0.75 = one person speaks everything when there are
four); top share = largest share; a turn = a run of consecutive segments by one person in start order;
turns per minute = all turns / meeting span; "started over" = began a segment while another person was
mid-segment (includes backchannels like "yeah").

Default meeting on the bench: **TS3012b** (functional design, 40.9 min). PM 45.3%, ME 18.4%, UI 16.8%,
ID 19.4%; Gini 0.217; 14.7 turns/min; turns PM 194, UI 117, ID 127, ME 165. Cap 30%: Gini 0.063 (PM 30.0,
ME 23.5, UI 21.9, ID 24.6). Cap 40%: 0.163. Loudest summarises: 0.255, PM 49.4%. Shuffle: 0.052.
Started over the PM: ID 125, ME 124, UI 72.

Other meetings worth naming: IS1001a (kick-off) PM 61.0%, Gini 0.416, cap 30% 0.105, loudest 0.429.
ES2009a PM 58.2%, Gini 0.380. ES2008b near-equal: Gini 0.044, top PM 28.8%.

**Honesty rails for every page:** the baseline is real; the cap is a stated redistribution rule, not a
prediction of a real round; the anti-lever is a model of what handing the close to one voice does to
the closing minutes; the AMI roles were assigned by the scenario and the PM role is told to run the
meeting, so PM dominance is partly the job description, which is itself the lesson (whoever holds the
process role tends to hold the floor). Inequality is not automatically bad: a kick-off where the PM
briefs the team is supposed to be unequal. The bench measures airtime, not quality of contribution.
