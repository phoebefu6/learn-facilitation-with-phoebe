### The Diamond of Participatory Decision-Making (read-at-source, chapter 1 excerpt, pp. 3-21)

- The chapter opens by contrasting an idealised picture (everyone tracks every idea at the same pace) with real groups that go on tangents and get attached to ideas.
- Divergent thinking vs convergent thinking are paired on p.6: generating vs evaluating alternatives, free-flowing open discussion vs summarizing key points, gathering diverse points of view vs sorting ideas into categories, suspending judgment vs exercising judgment.
- The early rounds of any discussion cover "familiar opinions"; most groups try to close every discussion that quickly (p.9). Hard problems need the group to break out of the narrow band of familiar opinions (p.10).
- Pp.16: a person-in-charge who announces a decision mid-confusion ("poor timing") can breed distrust and cynicism.
- P.18 "A realistic model": a period of confusion and frustration is presented as a natural part of group decision-making, labelled in the diagram as struggle in the service of integration.
- P.19 "Introducing the Groan Zone": struggling to understand foreign or opposing ideas is unpleasant; members can be repetitious, defensive, short-tempered. Simply naming the Groan Zone can help a group.
- P.20: the Diamond is credited as developed by Sam Kaner with Lenny Lind, Catherine Toldi, Sarah Fisk and Duane Berger. The diagram labels, read from the figure: **Business as Usual, Divergent Zone, Groan Zone, Convergent Zone, Closure Zone**, with "New Topic" at the left. The text lists three uses: a lens for observing meeting dynamics, a roadmap for agenda design, and a teaching tool giving members shared language.
- P.21: the Groan Zone is framed as a direct, inevitable consequence of diversity in a group, and working through it is what builds sustainable agreements. Short quote: "Building shared understanding is a struggle, not a platitude." (Kaner et al. 2014, p.20)
- Note for teaching: the book itself calls the end zone "Closure Zone" in the 2014 diagram. "Business as usual" is the zone where a familiar, quick decision happens before the diamond opens.

### Four participatory values (read-at-source, index of 3rd ed.)

The index lists, under "Values": full participation (pp.24, 25, 33), mutual understanding (pp.24, 26, 34), inclusive solutions, shared responsibility (pp.24, 28, 36). The index also lists, under "Facilitator": defined (p.32); encouraging full participation (33); promoting mutual understanding (34); fostering inclusive solutions (35); cultivating shared responsibility (36). So the four values map one-to-one onto four facilitator functions on pp.33-36. Chapter 2 is titled "Participatory Values" (p.23). I did not read the body text of chapter 2 or 3.

### Decision rules (read-at-source, index of 3rd ed.; body text not read)

Index entry "Decision rules, 324-325; common decision rules, 326" lists: **unanimous agreement (328), majority vote (329), flip a coin (329), person-in-charge decides after discussion (330), person-in-charge decides without discussion (331)**, plus "decision-making without decision rules, 327", "effects of decision rules on participation, 332", "reaching closure, 348". Also indexed: "Consensus: and unanimity, 334"; "silence is not an agreement, 335"; and a "gradients of agreement scale, 338".

- **Delegation** as a decision rule: commonly listed in secondary summaries of Kaner, but I did NOT find "delegation" in the 3rd edition index excerpt. Tier: **not verified** for the 3rd edition. Do not list it as Kaner's without reading p.326.

### Facilitator as content-neutral / process owner

- Exact definition text (p.32): **not verified** (body text not available).
- Reported: a widely circulated summary attributes to Kaner et al. the idea that the facilitator's job is to support everyone to do their best thinking, by encouraging full participation, promoting mutual understanding and cultivating shared responsibility (reported via Wikipedia "Facilitator" page and search snippets; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facilitator).
- The phrase "content neutral" is usually attributed to **Michael Doyle** (Doyle and Straus tradition; Doyle wrote the foreword to Kaner's 3rd ed.), not to Kaner. Tier: **reported** (Wikipedia "Facilitator", quoting Doyle). Teach it as "Doyle's phrase, a tradition Kaner's book sits in", not as a Kaner quote.

## 2. Google re:Work, Project Aristotle

### Citation

re:Work Editorial Team (originally published 2015). "Understand team effectiveness" (Guide). Google re:Work. https://rework.withgoogle.com/intl/en/guides/understand-team-effectiveness - **read-at-source** (fetched 2026-10-06; note the older URL `/en/guides/understanding-team-effectiveness` and the `/print/guides/5721312655835136/` URL both returned 404 or near-empty; use the `/intl/en/guides/understand-team-effectiveness` URL).

### What Google's own page says (read-at-source)

- Code name Project Aristotle; the page says Project Oxygen and Project Aristotle were released in 2011 and 2016 respectively.
- Sample: 180 teams (115 project teams in engineering and 65 pods in sales), a mix of high- and low-performing teams.
- Data: double-blind interviews with leaders, plus existing survey data including over 250 items from the annual engagement survey and gDNA.
- Headline: what mattered was less who is on the team, more how the team worked together.
- The five dynamics, listed "in order of importance": **Psychological safety, Dependability, Structure and clarity, Meaning, Impact**. Psychological safety described as by far the most important; teams with it are rated effective twice as often by executives (as printed: "twice as often").
- Variables NOT significantly connected to effectiveness at Google: colocation, consensus-driven decision making, extroversion, individual performance, workload size, seniority, team size, tenure. Google adds a caveat that these may still matter elsewhere.
- Credits Amy Edmondson (Harvard) for the "team psychological safety" construct.

### Does "equal conversational turn-taking" appear on Google's page?

**No.** I searched the full page text: zero occurrences of "turn", "speak", or "conversation" in the body text. Google's five dynamics do not include turn-taking.

### Where the turn-taking claim actually comes from

Duhigg, C. (2016, February 25 online; NYT Magazine issue of 28 February 2016). "What Google Learned From Its Quest to Build the Perfect Team." *The New York Times Magazine*. https://www.nytimes.com/2016/02/28/magazine/what-google-learned-from-its-quest-to-build-the-perfect-team.html - **read-at-source via Wayback Machine archived copy** (nytimes.com itself was blocked to my fetcher). Adapted from Duhigg's book *Smarter Faster Better* (2016).

What the article does:
- It describes **Woolley et al.'s** collective intelligence research (not Google's data) and says good teams spoke in roughly the same proportion, giving Woolley's term "equality in distribution of conversational turn-taking". It quotes Woolley that collective intelligence declined when one person or a small group spoke all the time.
- It then says researchers "sometimes colloquially" group turn-taking and average social sensitivity under psychological safety, and moves to Google's Aristotle findings on psychological safety.
- Short quote: "everyone had spoken roughly the same amount" (Duhigg 2016, describing Woolley's groups).

**Teaching line:** "Equal airtime" is a Woolley et al. (2010) correlate, popularised by Duhigg's NYT piece, which placed it alongside Google's psychological safety finding. Google's own guide lists five dynamics and does not mention turn-taking. Saying "Google found equal turn-taking makes teams effective" is a conflation.

## 3. Woolley, Chabris, Pentland, Hashmi, Malone (2010)

### Citation (read-at-source, PubMed record PMID 20929725)

Woolley, A. W., Chabris, C. F., Pentland, A., Hashmi, N., and Malone, T. W. (2010). Evidence for a collective intelligence factor in the performance of human groups. *Science*, 330(6004), 686-688. Published 29 Oct 2010 (epub 30 Sep 2010). doi:10.1126/science.1193147. PubMed: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20929725/ (read via NCBI E-utilities; science.org returned 403).

### What the abstract says (read-at-source)

- Two studies, **699 people**, working in **groups of two to five**.
- Converging evidence of a general collective intelligence factor explaining group performance across a wide variety of tasks; they call it the **"c factor"**.
- c is **not strongly correlated** with the average or maximum individual intelligence of members.
- c **is correlated** with (1) average social sensitivity of group members, (2) the equality in distribution of conversational turn-taking, and (3) the proportion of females in the group.
- Short quote: "not strongly correlated with the average or maximum individual intelligence" (Woolley et al. 2010, abstract).

### Numbers not in the abstract

- Number of groups per study: Bates and Gupta (2017) state Woolley et al. study 1 used **n = 40 groups**. Tier: **reported** (Bates and Gupta 2017, section on power). Study 2 group count: **not verified**.
- Social sensitivity measure: Reading the Mind in the Eyes test (Baron-Cohen et al. 2001). Tier: **reported** (Bates and Gupta 2017 describe Woolley's measures this way).
- Individual IQ measures: Raven's matrices or the Wonderlic Personnel Test. Tier: **reported** (Bates and Gupta 2017).
- Percent variance explained by the first factor, and the correlation coefficients for each correlate: **not verified** (paywalled article body). Do not quote numbers.
- PubMed links a formal comment: Credé and Howardson (2017), see topic 4.

## 4. The disagreement: does group IQ come from individual IQ or from process?

### Side A - Bates and Gupta (2017): it is mostly individual IQ

Bates, T. C., and Gupta, S. (2017). Smart groups of smart people: Evidence for IQ as the origin of collective intelligence in the performance of human groups. *Intelligence*, 60, 46-56. doi:10.1016/j.intell.2016.11.004. Volume/pages/year confirmed via Crossref. Abstract and body read from the accepted manuscript PDF (University of Edinburgh authors; copy hosted at https://www.gwern.net/doc/iq/2016-bates.pdf). Tier: **read-at-source**.

- Three studies, **312 people** in total.
- Individual IQ accounted for **around 80%** of group-IQ differences (as printed).
- Hypotheses that group-IQ rises with number of women and with turn-taking were **not supported**.
- Reading the Mind in the Eyes was associated with individual IQ and, in one study, with group-IQ factor scores; but a combined structural model (studies 2 and 3) showed RME had no influence on the latent group-IQ factor, only a modest impact on a single group test.
- In the structural model, individual IQ **determined 100%** of latent group-IQ (as printed).
- Body text: study 2 tested **40 groups** of 3 (matching Woolley's study 1 n of 40 groups); study 1 had 26 groups (12 of size 2, 8 of size 3, 6 of size 4). Turn-taking in study 2 was hand-coded from turns taken, not from Woolley's sociometric badges. Body text says all-male, all-female and mixed groups performed equally well, and no relationship of some members speaking more than others with group-IQ was seen.
- Short quote: "individual IQ accounted for around 80% of group-IQ differences" (Bates and Gupta 2017, abstract).

### Side A2 - Credé and Howardson (2017): the c factor itself is weak

Credé, M., and Howardson, G. (2017). The structure of group task performance - A second look at "collective intelligence": Comment on Woolley et al. (2010). *Journal of Applied Psychology*, 102(10), 1483-1492. doi:10.1037/apl0000176. PMID 29016184. Tier: **read-at-source** (abstract via PubMed). Note the real title: it is not "Revisiting the Collective Intelligence Factor".

- Re-examined data from **6 previously published samples**.
- Empirical support for the construct is "generally weak"; the general factor explains only little variance in performance on many group tasks.
- Two statistical artifacts may have inflated the covariation: apparent low-effort responding, and the nested nature of the data.
- Conclusion: insufficient support for a collective intelligence construct.
- Short quote: "insufficient support for the existence of a collective intelligence construct" (Credé and Howardson 2017, abstract).
- A reply exists: "Measuring Collective Intelligence in Groups: A Reply to Credé and Howardson", SSRN 3187373 (https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=3187373). Tier: **reported** (title seen in search results only; authors and year not verified; not read).

### Side B - Riedl, Kim, Gupta, Malone, Woolley (2021): a robust c, and process matters most

Riedl, C., Kim, Y. J., Gupta, P., Malone, T. W., and Woolley, A. W. (2021). Quantifying collective intelligence in human groups. *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 118(21), e2005737118. doi:10.1073/pnas.2005737118. PMID 34001598, PMCID PMC8166150. Erratum: PNAS 2022, 119(19), e2204380119. Tier: **read-at-source** (abstract via PubMed; body via PMC full text https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8166150/).

- **22 studies, 5,279 individuals in 1,356 groups** - confirmed exactly as printed.
- Supports a robust CI factor; CI predicts out-of-sample criterion tasks.
- CI predicted by the proportion of women in the group, **mediated by average social perceptiveness**.
- Overall, group collaboration **process** is more important in predicting CI than individual member **skill**; the significance statement orders them process, then individual skill, then group composition.
- **Critical precision:** in this paper "process" is **not turn-taking**. Process was measured from the computer-mediated POGS platform as three things: **skill congruence, strategy, and effort**. Skill congruence and strategy were strong positive predictors; effort was not significant. I found no turn-taking or speaking-equality measure in the PMC text.
- Relative importance was estimated with random forests. The paper says the process-vs-skill balance varies by task: more than 51% of explained variation on Sudoku was due to individual skill, while 55% on Unscrambling Words was due to group process (numbers as printed).
- Short quote: "group collaboration process is more important in predicting CI than the skill of individual members" (Riedl et al. 2021, abstract).

### How to teach the disagreement

1. Woolley 2010: c exists; weakly tied to member IQ; tied to social sensitivity, equal turn-taking, share of women.
2. Bates and Gupta 2017 (312 people, 3 studies): c is mostly member IQ (about 80%, up to 100% of latent group-IQ); turn-taking and women did not replicate; RME no effect on latent factor.
3. Credé and Howardson 2017 (6 samples re-analysed): the general factor itself is weak and possibly inflated by artifacts.
4. Riedl et al. 2021 (22 studies, 5,279 people, 1,356 groups; includes Woolley as an author): c is robust; process beats skill overall; women effect runs through social perceptiveness. But its "process" is skill congruence, strategy and effort in online tasks, **not** airtime equality.
5. Net for a facilitation course: "Equal airtime causes smarter groups" is not settled. What survives best: how a group coordinates (matching who does what to who is good at it, having a strategy) matters, and the size of that effect depends on the task. Equal turn-taking is one original 2010 correlate that a direct replication did not find.

### Corpus facts (read-at-source)

- **100 hours** of meeting recordings (home and overview pages).
- **Around two-thirds** elicited with a scenario (participants play roles in a design team taking a project from kick-off to completion over the course of a day); **the rest are naturally occurring meetings** in a range of domains.
- Signals: close-talking and far-field microphones, individual and room-view video cameras, slide projector output, electronic whiteboard, plus unsynchronised individual pens.
- Recorded in English in **three rooms** with different acoustic properties; **mostly non-native speakers**.
- Scenario page (https://groups.inf.ed.ac.uk/ami/corpus/scenariomeetings.shtml, text taken from McCowan et al. 2005): participants are employees of an electronics company designing a new **television remote control**. Roles: **project manager (PM)** coordinates, runs meetings, writes minutes and report; **marketing expert (ME)** user requirements, trends, evaluating the prototype; **user interface designer (UI)** technical functions and UI; **industrial designer (ID)** how the remote works including componentry. UI and ID jointly own look-and-feel. Participants were not trained designers; they received role training and a simulated personal coach by email.
- **Four phases, one meeting each:** project kick-off, functional design, conceptual design, detailed design. Each project done in one day.
- Meeting IDs page: for scenario meetings the suffix **a/b/c/d** = first to fourth meeting in the trial. Prefix letters: I Idiap, E Edinburgh, T TNO, B Brno; second letter S = remote control scenario, N = naturally occurring, B = other scenario elicitations (office move, movie club). Scenario sets: ES2002-ES2016 a-d; IS1001-IS1009 a-d except IS1002a and IS1005d; TS3003-TS3012 a-d.
- Role assignment: pilots with random roles performed poorly, so at TNO and Edinburgh participants chose roles. Familiarity between participants cannot be assumed.
- Participant IDs encode gender, site, and native language (English, Dutch, Other); TNO IDs append PM, ID, ME or UID.
- Non-scenario examples: EN2001 (MSc students, 4-5 participants), EN2002 (3-4 participants), IN10xx speech research colleagues at Idiap.

## Commonly misquoted or conflated claims

1. **"Google found that equal conversational turn-taking makes teams effective."** Google's re:Work guide lists five dynamics (psychological safety, dependability, structure and clarity, meaning, impact) and never mentions turn-taking. The turn-taking finding is Woolley et al. (2010), brought into the Google story by Duhigg's 2016 NYT Magazine article.
2. **"Collective intelligence is about equal airtime, not smart members."** One 2010 correlate. Bates and Gupta (2017) found turn-taking and number of women did not replicate and individual IQ explained around 80%. Credé and Howardson (2017) questioned the c factor itself.
3. **"Riedl et al. 2021 proved turn-taking still matters."** The meta-analysis found process beats skill overall, but its process measures were skill congruence, strategy and effort, not turn-taking. It confirms a women effect only via social perceptiveness.
4. **"Kaner's decision rules include delegation."** Not in the 3rd edition index I read; the indexed rules are unanimous agreement, majority vote, flip a coin, person-in-charge decides after discussion, person-in-charge decides without discussion. Check p.326 before adding others.
5. **"Content-neutral" as a Kaner quote.** The phrase is usually credited to Michael Doyle; Kaner's book sits in the same tradition (Doyle wrote its foreword).
6. **Credé and Howardson's paper title.** Often given as "Revisiting the Collective Intelligence Factor". Real title: "The structure of group task performance - A second look at 'collective intelligence': Comment on Woolley et al. (2010)".
7. **AMI licence as NonCommercial.** Older wording (NC-SA) survives in commented-out HTML and in old write-ups. The live licence is CC BY 4.0.
8. **"Tell me and I forget... involve me and I learn" - Benjamin Franklin.** The Liberating Structures 1-2-4-All page attributes it to Franklin. This attribution is widely reported as unsupported (Quote Investigator and others trace it elsewhere), but I could not open the Quote Investigator page (404). Tier: **reported / not verified**. Do not repeat the Franklin attribution on a course page.
9. **"The whole is greater than the sum of its parts" - Aristotle.** Google's page explains the Project Aristotle code name with this line attributed to Aristotle. It is commonly described as a paraphrase rather than an exact Aristotle quote. Tier: **not verified** here; avoid presenting it as a verbatim Aristotle quote.

## 7. Consent decision-making (added by course lead, read-at-source 2026-10-06)

Bockelbrink, B., Priest, J., and David, L. Sociocracy 3.0, pattern "Consent Decision-Making". https://patterns.sociocracy30.org/consent-decision-making.html - CC BY-SA 4.0. Read-at-source.

- Defined as a (facilitated) group process: invite objections, and use what they reveal to evolve the proposal.
- An objection is an argument that reveals consequences or risks preferably avoided, or a worthwhile way to improve. A concern is an assumption not (yet) backed by enough reasoning to be an objection.
- Proposals go ahead when "good enough for now and safe enough to try" until the next review (short quote).
